Window Light Direction, Diffusion, and Fill Angles for Small Rooms

By EMFLS Editorial Desk · Published November 19, 2026 · 7 min read (1,385 words)

A window can give you a bright cheek, a dark eye socket, and a hot strip on the nose within the same half metre of floor space. Mark the chair against the window, measure the light lost through a sheer, then move a white foam-core board until the cheek-to-cheek brightness gap lands around one to one and a half stops.

Lay out the room before metering

Start with a seated head-and-shoulders portrait in a room about 2.4 by 3 metres. The example uses a window with a 70 cm illuminated width and a height of about 1.2 m. Turn off ceiling lights and block any second window spilling onto the shadow cheek. If the main window throws a stripe of sun across the chair, move the chair out of the stripe or wait until the sheer covers the entire sunlit area before setting exposure.

Put the tripod about 1.3 m from the face, at seated eye height, and leave roughly 60 cm behind the chair if the room allows. Set the centre of the face 90 cm from the curtain plane, measured square to the window wall. Mark that perpendicular with tape. Use a second line from the face toward the camera. Keep the subject looking along that line during tests and mark the chair legs, since a small swivel changes which cheek sees the window.

Use manual exposure, fixed ISO, and fixed white balance for comparison frames. Lay out a tape measure, a matte gray card, and a 50 by 70 cm white foam-core board. Keep the board out of the room until the curtain and key direction are set. A white wall can still contribute fill, so record which walls and doors were exposed.

Measure the feather angle and window bearing

Identify the centre of the bright area visible from the chair and project it down to the floor. For an evenly lit sheer, use the middle of the uncovered width. Measure how far along the wall that point sits from your perpendicular tape mark. A 52 cm offset with a 90 cm perpendicular gap gives an angle of arctan(52 ÷ 90), or about 30° off the window normal. This guide calls that the feather angle. It describes how obliquely the face sees the window. In the sketch, the same window sits 60° from the subject-to-camera line because that line runs parallel to the wall.

For a second test, slide the chair and tripod together parallel to the wall until the offset is 24 cm. The feather angle is now about 15°, and the window bearing from the camera line is about 75°. Take both frames at the same exposure first. Compare the far eye, the edge of the nose shadow, and the highlight on the near cheek. At 75° the window is more lateral to the camera, so the far eye can lose its catchlight even when the curtain still lights the near cheek generously.

Choose the mark that keeps the far eye readable with the least fill. If you turn the face 10° toward the window from the 60° setup, the light is about 50° off the nose direction. Keep both values in your notes. The floor angle stays fixed through the head turn, while the light across the face changes.

Measure the sheer in stops

Place the gray card at cheek height, square to the camera, and hold it there for the whole test. With the board out, spot-meter the centre of the card or fill the metering area with it. Keep the aperture and ISO fixed. With the sheer open, record the shutter time that places the card at your chosen meter mark. Close the sheer across the whole opening, smooth out bunched fabric, and find the shutter time that returns the card to the same reading.

At ISO 200 and f/4, a change from 1/250 s to 1/125 s is a one-stop loss at the subject. A change from 1/250 s to 1/80 s needs about 3.1 times as much exposure time, a loss of roughly 1.6 stops. Reopen the sheer and repeat the first reading. If that reading has shifted by more than a third of a stop, wait for steadier daylight and run the sequence again.

Take a portrait with each curtain state at its matched exposure. Look at the transition across the nose shadow and the size and shape of the catchlight. A sheer over direct sun may turn a small hard source into a broad luminous area. Under overcast sky the bare window can already look soft, so the visible change may be mostly exposure loss. If the sheer pushes you below your working shutter speed, raise ISO and check a frame with the subject moving naturally. For a seated adult, 1/125 s is a useful trial setting when testing for small head movements.

Set the fill angle and distance

Stand the board on the shadow side, forward of the ear toward the camera, with its centre at cheek height. Start about 60° off the camera line on that side and 75 cm from the centre of the face to the centre of the board. Turn the white face until it sees both the window and the subject. If it sits almost edge-on to either, move or rotate it before judging distance. Keep the lower edge supported so the board can hold its angle by itself.

Lock the portrait exposure. Meter two cheek areas with similar reflectance, avoiding specular highlights, eye sockets, hair, and makeup transitions. If the meter spot is too broad, use two matching matte gray patches held in the cheek planes and meter from the camera direction. Subtract the shadow reading from the lit reading in EV. A gap of 1.5 stops corresponds to a brightness ratio of about 2.8 to 1 because 2 raised to 1.5 is 2.8. Each side reading includes all the light that reaches that surface.

Run the distances below in order and watch both readings. Move the board along the same bearing, then recheck its aim. At close range a large board covers much of the cheek's view, so halving the distance has no reliable fixed gain. If the window-side reading also rises, compare the new gap before deciding which distance works.

Verify the portrait and record the setup

Use the distance that gives the shadow cheek enough detail while keeping a clear nose and jaw shadow. If the board will only fit 35 cm from the face, rotate it away in 10° steps and re-meter. If glasses pick up a bright rectangle, move the board a little farther to the side or lift it, then read both cheeks again. A coloured wall can tint the shadow even at a good stop difference. Check the gray card in the shadow light and, if needed, cover the offending wall just outside the frame with a neutral cloth.

Take the final frame with the card removed, then zoom in on both eyes and inspect the brightest skin for clipping. Record the geometry and meter readings so you can rebuild the setup when the light returns.

  • Mark chair and tripod feet, the face-to-curtain gap, the along-wall offset, and the direction the subject faced.
  • Log open and sheer shutter readings at the same ISO and aperture, and check that the final open reading matches the first within a third of a stop.
  • Record board size, bearing, centre-to-face distance, and the final lit and shadow readings.
  • Check the far eye for detail, the near cheek for clipped highlights, and the frame edges for the board or its stand.

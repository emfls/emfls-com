Balancing Microphone Distance, Polar Patterns, and Proximity Effect

By EMFLS Editorial Desk · Published November 19, 2026 · 7 min read (1,357 words)

A close cardioid vocal can gain useful weight on held notes and still lose a take to one hard P. Record the same phrase on a dynamic and a condenser from 10 to 35 cm, then match playback levels and measure vowel bass and plosive peaks separately. The aim is a placement you can repeat that keeps the vocal full and the room in check.

Set the distance and angle references

Start at 20 cm with the mic in cardioid and its front axis pointed at the mouth. Use the manufacturer’s capsule or diaphragm reference if it is marked. If you can only measure to the grille, write down the point you used. An end-address dynamic and a side-address condenser can recess the capsule by different amounts. A 10 cm grille measurement on one mic may therefore put the diaphragm farther away than the same reading on the other. Measure with the singer in their working posture and pull the ruler away before recording.

Use two angles for two jobs. Moving the mic above or beside the breath stream and aiming it back at the mouth keeps the mouth on the mic’s front axis. Turning the mic so its axis misses the mouth makes the mouth off-axis, even if the capsule stays in the same place. The second move can reduce presence or change sibilance. Check the front mark before angling a side-address mic. See Shure’s recording techniques reference.

Record a controlled 10 to 35 cm sweep

Tape a mark for the singer and move the stand for 10, 15, 20, 25, and 35 cm. Keep the mic at mouth height for the initial on-axis sweep. Mount a pop filter about 5 cm in front of the capsule reference and move it with the mic. At 10 cm that leaves about 5 cm between lips and screen. If the grille prevents that spacing, choose a screen position that works across the sweep and log it. Keep the room, performer orientation, and screen type fixed.

Record one mic at a time. With filters, EQ, compression, and automatic gain disabled, have the singer deliver the loudest part at 10 cm and set the preamp for peaks around −12 dBFS. Keep that gain for the sweep and set it afresh for the other mic. Record three passes per distance with a sustained vowel at the same note, a phrase such as “Paul packed blue paper,” and two seconds of silence after the final word. Ask for consistent delivery at performance intensity. If the second pass at 20 cm sounds considerably thicker than the first, take another before drawing conclusions about the smaller distance steps.

Save the raw tracks. On playback copies, use clip gain to match RMS over a steady half-second of the vowel to within 0.5 dB. Use the same vowel and pitch for every match, and apply the gain change to the entire clip. Peak matching would let the worst pop turn down the rest of that take.

Measure vowel warmth and plosive peaks separately

On each matched vowel, use a band meter or the same analyzer settings to compare average energy in 100–250 Hz with 500–1500 Hz. Subtract the upper reading from the lower one. Use the median of the three passes and compare each distance with 35 cm on the same mic. If the note’s fundamental sits above 250 Hz, pick a lower test note the singer can sustain comfortably or adjust the low band for that voice and keep it fixed across takes. A pitch shift across a band edge can look like a change in bass.

Suppose the dynamic measures −5 dB for that band difference at 35 cm, −2 dB at 20 cm, and +1 dB at 10 cm. With 35 cm as the reference, those are +3 dB and +6 dB shifts in the low-to-mid balance. Those numbers illustrate the calculation. A room reflection or variation in delivery can also move the reading, so listen for a consistent change across passes and nearby distances.

For pops, meter a listening copy filtered to roughly 30–120 Hz. Compare the highest peak within a roughly 100 ms window around each hard consonant with the peak during the steady vowel, using the same filtered clip for both. If the filtered vowel peaks at −26 dBFS and the P hits −16 dBFS, the burst is 10 dB above that vowel. If the burst at 20 cm reaches −22 dBFS against the same vowel peak, its excess is 4 dB. Confirm the improvement by ear in the full-band recording. Check the unfiltered raw track for clipping or a sharp crackle, which can also come from overload earlier in the signal chain.

Compare the two cardioids and listen for the room

Cardioid describes directionality. Dynamic and condenser describe how the transducer works. Both cardioids can add bass up close, and their grilles, tuning, and off-axis response can lead to different preferred placements. A true pressure omni has essentially no proximity bass rise. If a condenser offers an omni setting, treat it as a separate comparison because the pattern switch can change both the tone and the amount of room sound.

In a free field, a point source would lose about 6 dB of direct level going from 10 to 20 cm and about 11 dB going from 10 to 35 cm. Use those figures to anticipate gain differences. The voice and reflective room can produce different readings. Once the takes are level matched, listen just after the phrase ends. A longer audible tail or a stronger reflection at 35 cm may cost more than the extra distance saves in plosive control. Turn the whole singer-and-mic setup relative to a nearby hard surface if reflections dominate, then repeat the finalists.

Keep the warmth and verify the working placement

Pick the shortest distance that sounds even across all three passes. If 15 cm has the vowel weight you want but the P bursts still jump out, move the mic about 5 cm above or to the side and aim it back at the mouth. Remeasure the straight-line distance. If bursts persist, try 20° off-axis at the same distance and listen for loss of articulation. At 20 cm the schematic’s angled placement puts the mouth about 6.8 cm sideways from the extended axis. Make one change per retake so you can hear which one helped.

If a clean placement still has excess rumble, audition a high-pass filter around 60–80 Hz in the mix and compare it at equal playback level. On a low voice, even that range can thin the bottom notes depending on the slope. If you still hear a hard thump or crackle after filtering, fix the airflow or overload at the mic and record the line again.

  • Record the loudest line and the quietest line at the chosen distance and angle.
  • Repeat with a natural 2 cm lean forward and back. Listen for bass swings, pops, and changes in diction.
  • Check the raw peaks, then confirm the matched vowel and consonant readings against the runner-up.
  • Listen in the mix and solo through the end of the phrase. Keep the take with usable vowel weight, clean consonants, and an acceptable room tail.
  • Log the mic model, pattern, measurement point, distance, angle, filter gap, preamp gain, and placement photo for the next session.

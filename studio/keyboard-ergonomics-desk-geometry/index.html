Measuring Chair Height, Monitor Angle, and Wrist Alignment for Long Sessions

By EMFLS Editorial Desk · Published November 19, 2026 · 7 min read (1,442 words)

A chair can feel right until the desk lifts your shoulders, the keyboard bends your wrists, or the screen makes you crane your neck. Use a tape measure to set loaded seat height and knee clearance, check elbow and wrist angles while typing, and align the monitor's top bezel with seated eye level. Record the measurements so you can restore the setup after someone moves it.

Set your reference points

Get a tape measure, a short rigid ruler, masking tape, and a phone camera or a second person. Use the same patch of floor for all vertical readings, and wear the shoes you normally work in. If the chair rolls onto a thick mat, measure everything from the mat’s top plane or account for its thickness. Measure the desk surface, the lowest obstruction underneath it, and the tops of the home-row keycaps separately. A 720 mm desktop with a 30 mm keyboard puts your fingers at 750 mm. A drawer can take away clearance at the same time.

Measure in the order the parts depend on one another. Set foot support and seat depth, put the keys within reach, then position the display. Sit back, type a few lines, and have a helper mark your eye height and measure the chair while you are still sitting. Standing to read the tape unloads the cushion. If you work alone, prop the phone square to your side and put the ruler in the same plane as the feature you want to measure. Keep the camera several steps back to reduce perspective error.

Fit the seat pan before raising the desk

Start with both feet supported and the backrest contacting your lower back. Measure vertically from the floor to the loaded seat surface beside your thigh, at a spot you can find again. Record that spot along with the height. An unloaded cushion at 468 mm might read 450 mm when you sit down. If the front edge digs into your thighs or your heels lift, lower the chair a small step and check again. If your knees rise sharply and your weight settles into a small patch at the rear of the cushion, try raising it slightly.

With your back still against the chair, measure the horizontal gap between the front edge of the cushion and the back of each lower leg just below the knee crease. About 50 mm is a useful starting point. If the smaller gap measures 18 mm, sliding the pan back by 30 mm would give roughly 48 mm if your hips stay put. Remeasure after moving it. If the pan ends well short of both knees and leaves your thighs poorly supported, try one notch forward and check for pressure as you move your feet. CCOHS chair adjustment guidance

Match the keys to your elbows and wrists

Drop your shoulders and let your upper arms hang close to your ribs. Bring your fingers to the home row without leaning forward. In a side photo, draw a line from shoulder to elbow and a second line from elbow to wrist. Read the inside angle between the rays that run from the elbow toward the shoulder and wrist. Start near 100°, within the 90° to 120° working range described by OSHA, and check that the shoulders stay relaxed when you reach for the top row. If the armrests hit the desk and push you back, lower them and recheck reach before changing heights. OSHA neutral working positions

Compare your seated elbow height with the keytops. If your elbow is at 705 mm and the keys are at 750 mm, try lowering the input surface by roughly 45 mm and take a new photo. At a fixed desk with room underneath, raising the chair 25 mm and using a stable 25 mm footrest can recover part of that difference. Recheck the gap between your thighs and the lowest obstruction before committing. Raising the seat by 25 mm uses up roughly that much clearance. If your thighs brush a tray or drawer when you move, change the input surface or obstruction before raising the chair further.

For wrist extension, use the side photo to extend the forearm’s long axis through the wrist. Compare it with a line from the wrist toward the middle-finger knuckle along the back of the hand. Use 0° to 5° of upward bend as a near-straight setup target. Over a 100 mm run along the forearm reference, a 7 mm perpendicular rise above that line is about 4°, and 9 mm is about 5°. Treat this as a coarse photo check. A few millimeters of landmark error can shift the reading by a couple of degrees. Fold down rear keyboard feet if they make your knuckles rise, and compare both wrists while typing. If the side view looks good but your wrists bend toward the little fingers, check from above and move or rotate the keyboard until the forearms approach the keys more directly.

Measure the monitor from your seated eye line

Look straight ahead with your head balanced and mark the height of your pupils on a nearby upright reference. Measure from the floor to that mark and to the center of the top bezel. An eye height of 1,185 mm and a bezel at 1,180 mm give a 5 mm drop. If you use progressive or bifocal lenses and lift your chin to read through them, lower the screen in small steps and test your actual text. Center the main display in front of your chair for work that stays on one screen. OSHA monitor positioning guidance

For the downward viewing angle, measure the horizontal run from the eye plane to the screen center and subtract screen-center height from eye height. With the center at 1,010 mm, the drop is 175 mm. Over a horizontal run of 650 mm, arctangent of 175 divided by 650 gives about 15°. A tape pulled directly from eye level to screen center would measure the sloping distance instead. Adjust the panel’s physical tilt separately. With a 330 mm vertical separation between top and bottom measurement points, moving the top 58 mm farther away than the bottom gives about 10° of backward tilt from vertical. Try the tilt with your work lights on. If it catches a lamp reflection, bring the panel closer to vertical or move the light, then recheck bezel height. Enlarge text if you lean in to read.

The final readings illustrate the changes you might measure after fitting the chair and lowering the input surface. Record your own baseline and final values in the same units so you can reproduce the setup on a shared bench.

Verify the setup on a real task

Spend ten minutes on work you actually do. Alternate typing with pointing, read a dense panel of text, and reach for a shortcut you use often. Check again when you settle into your usual posture. A good measurement taken while you hold still can miss a lamp reflection, a mouse that sits too far out, or a desk edge you lean against during edits.

  • Confirm that both feet stay supported and the front edge of the seat clears both knees as you shift.
  • Slide each leg forward and back. Check for contact with drawers, rails, and tray hardware.
  • Check both wrists at the home row and on the pointing device. Move the mouse inward if your elbow drifts away from your side.
  • Read the top and bottom of the screen without lifting your chin or leaning forward. Recheck the bezel after changing chair height or recline.
  • Record loaded seat height, pan notch, keytop height, footrest height if used, and bezel height. Keep the readings where you can reach them during the next reset.

During long sessions, change posture and get up between work blocks. When you return, use your measurements to restore the setup if it has moved.
